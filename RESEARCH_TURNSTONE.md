# Turnstone (Bellingcat) — research and decision

Researched 2026-08-23 against [`bellingcat/adsb-history`](https://github.com/bellingcat/adsb-history)
(MIT, 89 stars, created 2025-08-22, last push 2026-05-25) and the launch article
[*Using Bellingcat's New Open Source Tool to Explore Historical and Spatial Flight Data*](https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2026/03/05/turnstone-flight-tracking-tool/),
5 March 2026, by Logan Williams.

---

## The headline: a correction to our own foundations

**This project has been documenting a limit that does not exist.**

`ARCHITECTURE.md` and `DATA_SOURCES.md` state that adsb.lol keeps roughly 40 days and that
deep backfill is therefore impossible. That was measured honestly — the live trace endpoint
really does return 504 beyond about 40 days — and then generalised to the whole provider
without checking whether the data lived anywhere else. It does.

Turnstone's README points at it in one line: data comes from
[adsb.lol's data releases](https://github.com/adsblol/globe_history_2025/releases).

Verified today:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| `adsblol/globe_history_2024` | daily releases, ~2.1 GB each |
| `adsblol/globe_history_2025` | daily releases, ~3.2 GB each |
| `adsblol/globe_history_2026` | daily releases, ~3.3–4.1 GB each, current to yesterday |

Each release is a `tar` split into 2 GB parts, containing `./heatmap/` and
`./traces/<last2hex>/trace_full_<hex>.json` — **the same trace files the live endpoint
serves.** Confirmed by extracting `505c06` for 2026-08-15 from the archive and comparing
it with the copy we collected live: identical SHA-256, 157 716 bytes, 806 trace points,
byte-for-byte the same.

This is the second time in this project that I have concluded "it does not exist" from a
single negative probe — the first was the Slovak Air Force Global 5000s, absent from the
civil register because they are in the military one. The pattern is worth naming: *absence
from the source I happened to check is not absence.* It is exactly the failure the
coverage methodology exists to prevent, applied to everything except my own research.

### What it costs to use

The tar entries are **not** in sorted order (observed sequence of trace directories: 20,
65, cb, 50, 02, 87, e7, fa, a1…), so a filename binary search over HTTP range requests is
impossible, even though the CDN does support ranges. The only way in is to stream the
archive and keep what you want.

Measured on one day, 2026-08-15, extracting six aircraft:

```
3.75 GB streamed · 75 987 tar entries · 173 seconds
```

That is entirely workable as a slow backfill: a scheduled job processing eight to fifteen
historical days per run fills two years of history within a couple of months, at no
storage cost to us — we keep only our own aircraft, which is about 5 MB per year.

## What Turnstone actually is

Three components, MIT licensed:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| `backend-data-loading` | Python; parses tar1090 **heatmap** binaries into PostgreSQL + PostGIS |
| `backend-api` | Flask REST API, Firebase authentication |
| `frontend` | Vue 3 + interactive map, query builder, CSV export, IndexedDB query history |

One flat table:

```sql
adsb(t, hex, flight, alt, gs, geom GEOMETRY(Point,4326), bearing,
     registration, typecode, category, military)
```

plus a `modes` lookup keyed by hex. Endpoints: `/api/adsb/bbox`,
`/api/adsb/intersect_bboxes` (aircraft present in two areas within a time window),
`/api/adsb/hex_list`. Filters on time, altitude, speed, bearing, category, typecode,
military.

### The decisive technical difference

**Turnstone ingests heatmaps. We need traces.**

readsb heatmap files are decimated position samples. They carry no on-ground flag, no
vertical rate, and coarser timing. Our flight detection is built on exactly those fields:
the ground flag decides where a flight begins and ends, vertical rate and the 1.7-second
median interval are what let block time be measured rather than assumed.

Feeding our detector heatmap data would silently degrade every flight, every duration and
every cost that follows from them. This single fact settles most of the decisions below.

### One thing to be careful about

`modes.csv` is tar1090-db augmented with `category` and `military` columns **generated by
a large language model** from the type description. Their README says so plainly, to their
credit. Under our source hierarchy that is tier D — an analytical estimate. Useful as a
hint for finding aircraft worth investigating; not something to state as fact about an
aircraft's status. Our own registry rejected the OM-BH* Black Hawks precisely because a
plausible-looking military categorisation was wrong.

## Decision

### A — Reuse directly

* **The adsb.lol daily release archive as a data source.** The most valuable thing this
  research produced. It removes the project's biggest limitation.
* **`modes.csv`** as an aircraft-metadata source, for registration/type/owner. MIT, and
  richer than the raw tar1090-db we use now. The LLM-derived `category` and `military`
  columns come in as tier D and may never be published as fact.

### B — Adapt

* **The two-area intersection query.** "Which aircraft were in area A and later in area B"
  is a genuinely good primitive we do not have. Worth adapting when spatial analytics
  arrive — but as our own SQL, because we deliberately have no PostGIS and a nearest-airport
  grid index in TypeScript has served this fleet fine.
* **Their category vocabulary** (airliner / business jet / general aviation / UAV) as a
  useful axis, sourced properly rather than inherited from a model's guess.

### C — Methodological inspiration only

* **Baseline-then-anomaly framing.** Their tanker example — establish what normal traffic
  looks like, then notice the week that is not normal — is directly applicable to a state
  fleet, and better than the "top routes" list we currently produce.
* **Query history and CSV export** in the frontend.
* **Their honesty about the LLM-derived columns**, which is a standard worth matching.

### D — Keep our own

Everything above raw positions, because Turnstone does not attempt it and is not trying to:

```
flight reconstruction · airport matching · mission grouping · purpose from sourced
documents · cost engine · commercial comparison · break-even · publication delay
```

Three of our constraints are structural and rule out adopting Turnstone as the base:

1. **Traces, not heatmaps** — see above.
2. **The publication delay.** Turnstone is an investigator's console: log in, query
   anything, including this morning. Ours is a public site that must never reveal a
   current position. Bolting a delay onto a general query API is the wrong shape; ours is
   a gate in one function with tests around it.
3. **No server.** Turnstone needs PostgreSQL + PostGIS, Flask and Firebase. Ours builds a
   static page in CI with no credentials and no database facing the internet. Adopting
   Turnstone's stack would mean running and securing a server for a site that has no
   reason to be dynamic.

---

## Where Turnstone is still worth running

Not as our foundation — as a **research console beside it**. Our pipeline knows five
aircraft deeply; Turnstone would answer questions about the sky as a whole:

* which other aircraft visit Bratislava on the days our fleet flies
* whether a state aircraft's route is unusual against the baseline for that corridor
* finding airframes we have not yet added to the registry

That is a separate deployment on a separate machine, not part of the public site.

### Storage, if that is ever done

An estimate with its basis, not a figure to rely on. One global day is 3.75 GB compressed
in the archive. A Europe bounding box is perhaps a quarter to a third of world traffic, so
roughly 1 GB compressed per day; in PostgreSQL with a GIST index on the geometry, expect
several times that on disk. Order of magnitude: **1–3 TB per year for Europe**, which is
why ingesting Europe-wide is not something to do casually, and why our own store — five
aircraft, about 5 MB a year — stays as it is.

## What follows from this

1. Correct `ARCHITECTURE.md` and `DATA_SOURCES.md`: the 40-day limit belongs to the live
   endpoint, not to the provider.
2. Add an `AdsbProvider` for the release archive, alongside the live one. Same interface,
   same normalised `AdsbPosition`, so nothing downstream changes.
3. Backfill slowly in CI — a handful of historical days per run, oldest first.
4. Re-check the tracked aircraft against the archive era: **9513 and 9633 entered Slovak
   service around December 2024**, so their Slovak history starts there and their earlier
   flights under previous marks carried different ICAO addresses. Time-aware identity
   matters as soon as history goes back that far.
5. Keep prioritising ICAO hex over callsign, and keep treating a missing day as unknown
   rather than as a day without flights. Neither changes; both matter more with two years
   of data than with forty days.
